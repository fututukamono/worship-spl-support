How loud should church worship be?

By Daisuke Kawaue, maker of Worship SPL. Published October 1, 2026. Sources checked October 1, 2026.

Short answer: published numbers

SourceNumberHow it is measured
Life.Church (one large US church)About 93-94 dBA average over 18 minutes of worship; quick peaks under 99-100 dBA. The same page also mentions a desired average of 91-92 dBA.A-weighted, slow response (dBA-slow), monitored with Smaart
Behind The Mixer (a church sound engineer)About 86 dBA at one church and 91 dBA at another. He also knows teams running around 104 dBA.dBA, slow response
NIOSH (US workplace guidance)85 dBA averaged over an eight-hour workday; halve the time for every 3 dBA increase.A workplace limit, not a church rule

Behind The Mixer puts it plainly: "I won't recommend a specific volume level because it all depends on your room and the congregation." Life.Church says its numbers "fall within OSHA standards."

Why the average matters more than the peak

Using the NIOSH rule, the time at a level shrinks quickly as the level rises:

Average levelTime at that level (NIOSH, 3 dBA rule)
85 dBA8 hours
88 dBA4 hours
91 dBA2 hours
94 dBA1 hour
97 dBA30 minutes
100 dBA15 minutes

Calculated by us from the NIOSH recommended exposure limit of 85 dBA and the 3 dBA exchange rate. A worship set is shorter than a workday; this table only shows why teams watch the average of a whole set.

What to measure

  • Write it the same way every time. Behind The Mixer writes levels like "86dBA(slow)": the number, the weighting and the response.
  • Measure where people sit. The congregation hears the room, not the mixer position.
  • Use the meter to record what works. Behind The Mixer: "a meter should not be used as the primary means of setting and maintaining the volume."

What about phone apps?

Life.Church is direct about this:

"There are several applications available on mobile devices to help visualize this average. These apps are typically not very accurate. If an attendee is attempting to monitor SPL using their phone, remember it is not a valid measurement tool."

Behind The Mixer adds that smartphone meters "are also often limited to 100 dB and rely on the phone's microphone."

Our own view, as the maker of a phone app: a phone should not replace a sound level meter. What a phone at the mixer can do is give you the same measurement every week after you calibrate it once against a real meter.

Keep a weekly record

When someone says it was too loud, or too quiet, the strongest thing a team can show is what was measured each week, measured the same way. A weekly log of the level, the 15-minute average and the time over your church's limit gives that conversation a starting point.

Worship SPL is an iPhone app for this weekly log. Calibrate it once with a sound level meter. It then estimates the 15-minute average at the center of the seating area, compares it with your church's limit, and shares a one-page report with your sound team. Free to download; logging and reports need a subscription with a 14-day free trial. Note that Worship SPL uses an energy average over 15 minutes, so the dBA-slow figures above are not limits you can copy directly.
